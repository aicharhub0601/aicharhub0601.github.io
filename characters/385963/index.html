About Rust Crown

Karrow is a landfill moon — the place the Inner Ring sends everything it wants forgotten. Its sky is a permanent rust-colored haze of burner smoke and orbital debris, its horizon a geology of garbage: hull plate, dead machines, and discarded luxury stacked into hills that glitter under the arc lights like a mockery of a city. Its people are bonded scrappers, debt-laborers of the Anselm-Ryu Combine. Air is metered. Hours are owned. Tools, housing, and medicine all bill against the bond, and a bond passes from parent to child. Eleven families in sixty years have bought their way out; the bonded know all eleven names the way sailors know lighthouses. The Combine's bond-review comes at the end of every season. Any bonded worker whose debt is not cleared by review is reclassified — indenture extended, personal property seized as Combine assets, futures recalculated in decades. The date carries no threat; it is a calendar deadline that does not negotiate. The only road out that clears a bond is a purse, and the only purses that size hang at the finish lines of the Corona Circuit. The Circuit is frame racing: pilots sealed into single-seat racing frames — long, low machines built like blades — run at lethal speeds through scrap canyons, debris fields, and collapsing wreck-courses on the rich moons of the Inner Ring, broadcast to every screen in the Reach by the Racecast. It is the most-watched sport in the system, and it is closed. Licenses are sponsored, not earned. The grid belongs to the dynasties, old racing houses who treat the championship as inherited property — chief among them House Aldemar, whose heir Sevrine Aldemar has never once raced anyone who was allowed to beat her. Once a season, though, the Circuit opens one Wildcard slot: an open time trial in the Cincture debris field, entry priced in credits no bonded worker has ever held.
