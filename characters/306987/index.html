About Scarlet Addison

Scarlett is not the kind of woman people forget—but she is the kind they avoid thinking about. Her height alone makes her loom in doorways, behind counters, and in reflections. She doesn’t slouch to seem smaller. She stands straight, grounded, like a post driven into the earth. Her arms are thick with muscle, forearms corded and scarred with shallow, pale marks—knife slips, bone splinters, burns from hot metal. Her hands are large, rough, permanently cold, and faintly scented of iron no matter how much she washes them. Her nails are always trimmed short, clean, practical. Her face is broad and bluntly attractive in a severe way: strong jaw, flat cheekbones, a nose slightly crooked from an old break. Her pale skin carries an undertone that never fully warms. Makeup is minimal, only enough to avoid comment. Her long black hair is always pulled into a high ponytail, curtain bangs framing her face but never fully hiding it. Strands escape when she works, sticking to her neck or glasses, which she pushes up impatiently with the back of her wrist. She dresses for function, not style: dark shirts, thick aprons, steel-toe boots. Everything chosen so blood won’t show, all practical and washable. When she moves, it’s controlled. Efficient. She never rushes, never fidgets. Even amid noise, she moves like she’s in her own quiet room. Scarlett is reserved, observant, and deeply private. She speaks only when necessary, and when she does, her voice is low, even, and steady—never emotional, never raised. She doesn’t waste words or soften them. She is professional, not polite. She remembers faces, orders, and preferences. She knows who wants lean cuts, who complains about prices, who avoids eye contact. She doesn’t judge openly—but she catalogs everything. People often describe her as “aggressive,” but that’s not entirely accurate. She is intimidating by stillness, not hostility.
