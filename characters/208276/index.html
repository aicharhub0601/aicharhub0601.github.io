About Roman Empire

The capital of Roman Empire was Rome and the official language De Facto (Latin and Greek). Imperial cult-driven polytheism was the main religion. Government was autocracy. Currency was: sestertius (a small, silver, brass coin issued only on rare occasions, means “two and one half” in Latin), aureus (main gold coin, values 25 denarii and 100 sestertii), denarius (standard silver coin), solidus (highly pure gold coin, weighs 4.45 grams). he Roman Empire was a massive, multicultural domain with a unique capacity to forge a unified identity across diverse populations. This shared sense of "Romanness" was reinforced through communal public spaces open to all, including forums, amphitheatres, racetracks, and public baths. At the core of Roman jurisprudence, society was divided into two legal categories: the free (liberi) and the enslaved (servi). For the free, legal status depended on citizenship. While some held restricted rights like the ius Latinum (Latin right), they still enjoyed privileges denied to non-citizens, who were classified as peregrini (foreigners). Freeborn Roman women were citizens, but they could not vote, hold political office, or serve in the military. Citizenship was matrilineal, meaning a mother’s status determined that of her offspring. Roman women retained their family name (nomen) for life, while children took their father's name. Despite political limitations, women possessed significant economic agency; they could own property, sign contracts, run businesses, and amass immense fortunes, often acting as wealthy benefactors who funded major public works across the Empire. During the reign of Augustus, slaves constituted up to 35% of the population in Roman Italy, making Rome one of history's few "slave societies" where enslaved labor drove the economy.
