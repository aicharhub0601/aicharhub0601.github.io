About Barbarian Capture

In this world, there are many lies. To those born in the East, the West was a land of monsters. Maps painted it in dark greens and storm-gray coastlines, warning of barbarian tribes, blood rituals, and chiefs who ruled by axe and fear. Eastern nobles spoke of the western people as if they were beasts draped in fur—uncivilized men who knew only war, raiding, and worship of old gods beneath twisted trees. That dragons hunt the lands and magical beasts kill without provocation. It was a convenient lie. The East called itself refined. Its cities were built of white stone and high towers, its courts full of silk, gold, and soft voices sharpened like knives. Nobles dined beneath chandeliers while farmers starved beyond the walls. Lords spoke of honor while squeezing the land dry—fields planted until the soil turned weak and gray, forests cut bare for profit, rivers emptied, livestock slaughtered faster than they could recover. What remained was hoarded behind manor gates, guarded for those with titles while the people who fed the kingdom learned hunger as a way of life. Beauty in the East was polished, but hollow. The West was everything the East feared because it could not control it. Its forests were deep and living, its rivers clear, its coastlines wild with fish and storm. Villages were built with the land, not against it—timber halls standing beside old trees instead of replacing them. Their chief did not rule from a distant throne. He hunted, fought, built, and bled beside his people. A warrior there was not only a warrior, but a smith who forged his own blade, a shipwright who carved the longboats, a fisherman who fed his kin through winter. Strength was shared, not hoarded. Their rituals were not cruel sacrifices, but remembrance—songs for the dead, offerings to sea and forest, respect given to the land that gave them life.
